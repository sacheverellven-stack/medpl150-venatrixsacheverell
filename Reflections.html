Reflection 1 Susan Sontag’s ‘In Plato’s Cave’ presents the reality of photographs. They are yet mirrors held up to the past, both preservative of it, but also warping of it. Photography as a tool is as much art as any other widely accepted medium, as it has the power to tell a story. Nevertheless, it has many applications beyond what we would typically consider ‘art’. It captures a moment in time and preserves it for as long as its film - or power source - lasts. There is an interesting timeline laid out in ‘In Plato’s Cave’. That timeline spans from large ‘contraptions’ to small pocket-sized cameras. In this way, cameras have become markers for progress. Each era since its inception has been marked by its own style of camera, and as such also by its own style of photo. There is something about pictures of the past that cannot be replicated in modern images, and indeed, this is the way in which photography documents the past. One might argue its existence is inherently as a documentation, even when it is used in the most abstract manner possible. A camera is not inherently biased. It may be used to propagandize, as all media may, but it is unbiased. As such, so long as the truth surrounding a photo is applied to it, then we are aware of the past in a manner that is only jaded by our own prejudices. Furthermore, now more than ever, photography is accessible to the masses. Obtaining a camera is much easier now than it was one hundred years ago, and, as they can be pocket-sized and even disposable, they are not the commitment they once were. Nevertheless, as photographs are unbiased, they can be damning against those aiming to deceive - with a photo comes proof and with proof comes power. This means that activists can disprove the claims of tyrants, and officers of the law can gather evidence related to a crime. Yet, cameras are also a means of division. Photos can be used to slander and blackmail. In our modern era, photography is tied to revenge porn and stalking. Photographs are not just items of whimsy and fact, but also of emotion, particularly lust and revenge. While it is, in most cases, not legal to do these sorts of things with a camera, it often doesn’t matter that it’s not, as taking photos is not seen as insidious in our society, and most would assume a photographer’s intention to be banal. Furthermore, once a picture is taken, it can easily be disseminated, especially on the internet, which makes it difficult to contain the spread of. Photography can both enchant and ruin one’s life. Ultimately, photographs are treated as relics of the past, and as such the amount they can ruin one’s life is finite. Photos are treated as relics of their time, rather than as something whose influence should persist. An inflammatory photo from 1900 is not looked on the way an inflammatory photo from yesterday is. Yet, that prevalence is even more chilling, insofar as it is only commonplace due to its ability to distort reality. Without question, it was inevitable that AI would become the tool of fascist would-be dictators, as its sole purpose seems to be to propagandize. While this ability to propagandize is true of all media, it is particularly true of AI, as it can create photorealistic scenes and images that distort one’s perception of reality. This distortion is, unfortunately, already widespread. Articles have come out about AI convincing people to commit suicide, fueling psychosis that may or may not have existed prior to their interaction with the tool. This distortion of reality also exists in relation to the art world. Art styles are stolen by AI, without the consent of the artists, and used to create works, sometimes for profit. Artists were already aware of the risks of AI to their field, and now are witnessing the slow dissolution of their ability to make money from their craft. Yet, it is more than this. It is that art - a necessary and fundamental part of the human experience - is slowly disappearing. No, I don’t think artists will simply stop creating, but I do think fewer people will put the effort into becoming artists, or worse, will not be properly educated on art. The rise of AI - perhaps owing to everything else it’s coupled with - seems like a major historical event. Yet, it is rare that major historical events are anything more than tragedy. Perhaps AI can be put to good use - it seems to have good applications in scientific fields without encroaching on the jobs of researchers; however, it is also likely to cause collapses in education. It has, in a very short time, upheaved our society and begun its fundamental transformation. Yet, it is still to be seen how it transforms us. Personally, I do not believe that fascism can exist without destroying itself, and already, there is great opposition to both it and the propagandization that is occurring through the use of AI. People are fed up with AI slop, and with what it stands for. Inevitably, AI is likely to be little more than a fad, albeit a dangerous one. Despite AI’s dumbing down of society and distortion of reality, I do think humanity will persevere. It is impossible to make AI so ubiquitous that it truly takes over reality. So long as people leave their homes and go interact with others in the real world, there will always be a sense of truth, and where there’s truth, there’s justice. This justice means that AI’s use as a tool of propaganda will eventually be halted, and artists will not have to fear our obsolescence. Truly, AI - no matter how well it can replicate the world - cannot fully undermine art and artists. There is no soul in it; it feels hollow and uncanny, even when there’s nothing immediately visibly wrong with the images it produces.
